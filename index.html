1

Introduction to teaching infants and toddlers

Why the first three years matter and the ideas that guide our practice.

The brain builds its basic architecture in the first three years, and it does this through relationships. Each responsive exchange between a baby and a caring adult strengthens new connections (Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University, n.d.-a). Teaching this age group is relational work: feeding, settling, nappy changes and floor play are all curriculum.

In Australia this work is guided by the Early Years Learning Framework V2.0 (Australian Government Department of Education [AGDE], 2022) and the National Quality Standard (Australian Children's Education & Care Quality Authority [ACECQA], 2024). EYLF V2.0 asks educators to help very young children "build secure attachments with one and then other familiar educators" (AGDE, 2022, p. 31).

Bowlby, 1969; Ainsworth et al., 1978/2015

A consistent caregiver who responds warmly gives babies a secure base to explore from.

Powell et al., 2014

Children go out to explore and come back for comfort, so teachers stay available at both points.

Across all of these, we see infants as active people with their own rights and voices (Dalli et al., 2011).

2

Teaching infant and toddler education

Contemporary pedagogy and the strategies teachers use every day.

Most teaching with under-threes happens one to one, often in the middle of a routine, and it depends on the teacher being close and tuned in (Degotardi & Pearson, 2014). EYLF V2.0 practices such as responsiveness to children, play-based learning and intentionality, and continuity of learning and transitions all apply (AGDE, 2022). We put them into action through:

  • Primary caregiving, where one key person leads the care of a small group so each child forms a secure bond (Elfer et al., 2012).
  • Responsive, unhurried care: watch first, narrate, then wait for the child's response (Gonzalez-Mena & Eyer, 2025).
  • Intentional teaching and scaffolding, such as adding a ramp when a toddler keeps rolling balls.
  • Sustained shared thinking with older toddlers (Siraj-Blatchford et al., 2002).
  • The planning cycle of observing, planning, acting and reflecting, with families' knowledge included.

Routines across the day

Arrival

The key person greets the family by name and takes the child at their pace.

This takes time, which is one reason the National Regulations set a ratio of one educator to four children from birth to 24 months, and one to five from 24 to 36 months (ACECQA, 2024).

3

Infant and toddler play environments

Guidelines for rooms and outdoor spaces that feel safe, calm and interesting.

An infant and toddler room should feel more like a calm home than a busy classroom. Reggio Emilia educators describe the environment as a teacher in its own right (Edwards et al., 2012). Good rooms have clear floor space, natural light, low open shelves and a small number of open-ended natural materials. Noise lowers quality for under-twos, so we keep sound and clutter down (Dalli et al., 2011). Babies are never propped into positions they cannot reach themselves (Tardos, 2010), and anything smaller than a D-size battery is a choking hazard (ACECQA, n.d.).

Baby nest

Soft floor space for non-walkers with an unbreakable mirror and textured tummy-time mats.

Select another area on the plan to read about it.

If toddlers keep carrying things around (a transporting schema), we add bags and carts instead of stopping them (Athey, 2007).

4

Promoting infant and toddler development, learning and wellbeing

Broad strategies that work across every domain.

Development, learning and wellbeing are tied together. A baby who is hungry, tired or frightened cannot learn well, so wellbeing comes first (AGDE, 2022). Our broad strategies across every domain are:

  • Secure relationships through a key person, consistent staff and predictable routines.
  • Following the child's lead, planning from observed interests and schemas.
  • Play as the main way of learning, with teachers as co-players who extend without taking over.
  • Language all day: naming, narrating and taking turns.
  • Daily movement, with at least 30 minutes of tummy time for babies, three hours of active play for toddlers and no screen time under two (Australian Government Department of Health, 2021).
  • Inclusion, adapting spaces so children with disability or additional needs take part fully.
  • Cultural safety: home-language words, family cultures and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives embedded with local community guidance (AGDE, 2022).
  • Partnerships with families, who are children's first teachers.
  • Early identification, using milestone checklists to start conversations and refer early (Zubler et al., 2022).

Sections 5 to 9 apply these strategies to each domain (HASS means Humanities and Social Sciences).

5

Supporting cognitive development

How babies and toddlers think, remember and solve problems.

Cognitive development is how children learn to think, remember and solve problems. Babies learn by mouthing, dropping and watching what happens (Piaget, 1952). Near their first birthday they search for hidden objects and show early executive function (Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University, 2011). Toddlers repeat play patterns called schemas, like transporting and enclosing (Athey, 2007), testing ideas like little scientists (Gopnik et al., 1999).

  • Observe closely and recognise schemas and problem-solving in play.
  • Scaffold within the zone of proximal development, giving just enough help (Vygotsky, 1978).
  • Wonder aloud and ask open questions, such as where did it go?
  • Offer open-ended materials and long, uninterrupted play (Hughes, 2016).

Fostering social development

How young children connect, cooperate and belong.

Social development is how children learn to connect, cooperate and belong. Newborns already join in exchanges of looks and sounds (Trevarthen & Aitken, 2001). Between 9 and 15 months babies begin pointing and following an adult's gaze to share attention (Carpenter et al., 1998). By 18 months toddlers help others without reward (Warneken & Tomasello, 2006). Toddlers also form early friendships, often by copying each other (Degotardi & Pearson, 2014).

  • Model kind words and narrate what other children feel or do.
  • Set up small groups of two or three for peer play (HeadStart.gov, 2024).
  • Coach turn-taking without forcing toddlers to share before they are ready.
  • Guide behaviour by redirecting and explaining, never shaming.
  • Learn each family's culture so every child belongs (Rogoff, 2003).

7

Fostering emotional development

How children experience, express and begin to manage feelings.

Emotional development is how children experience, express and learn to manage feelings. Babies rely on adults to help them calm, and a secure attachment gives them a safe base (Bretherton, 1992). Still-face studies show how quickly babies become distressed when a familiar adult stops responding (Tronick et al., 1978). Separation anxiety often peaks around 14 to 18 months (Raising Children Network, n.d.-b), and tantrums are common from 18 months to three years (Healthdirect Australia, 2023).

  • Tune in to each child's cues and respond quickly and calmly.
  • Co-regulate by staying close and comforting before expecting self-control.
  • Name and accept feelings, the core of emotion coaching (Gottman et al., 1996; Gus et al., 2015).
  • Keep routines predictable and settle children gradually, with comfort items from home (Raising Children Network, n.d.-b).

8

Facilitating language and literacy development

From first coos to conversations, stories and marks on paper.

Language starts long before first words. Newborns already prefer their mother's voice (DeCasper & Fifer, 1980), and most children say first words between 12 and 18 months (Starting Blocks, 2026). Back-and-forth conversation matters more than how many words children hear (Romeo et al., 2018; Gilkerson et al., 2018), and babies learn speech from live, responsive people far better than from screens (Kuhl, 2004). Literacy grows alongside talk through stories, songs and mark-making.

  • Answer every coo, gesture and word, then wait for the child's turn.
  • Talk about what the child is already looking at (Tomasello & Farrar, 1986).
  • Expand and extend, so dog becomes big dog, then the big dog is running.
  • Read daily, using dialogic reading prompts with toddlers (Huebner & Meltzoff, 2005).
  • Use home-language words and Key Word Sign alongside speech (Cologon & Mevawalla, 2018).

Perceptual development and the five senses

How the brain learns to make sense of what children see, hear, touch, taste and smell.

Perception is how the brain makes sense of the senses. Hearing, taste and smell work at birth, while vision is least mature: newborns focus at about 25 centimetres and see almost normally by 8 months (SickKids Staff, 2024). Gibson (1988) argued that babies learn to perceive by exploring what objects let them do, and in the visual cliff study most crawling babies avoided an apparent drop (Gibson & Walk, 1960). Babies learn best when sight and sound arrive together (Bahrick & Lickliter, 2000).

Sight

Faces and contrast first, colours from about 4 months.

  • Offer sensory-rich rooms that do not overwhelm, rotating a few materials at a time.
  • Watch for sensory preferences and signs of overload (Raising Children Network, n.d.-a).
  • Risk-assess materials: no water beads for under-threes (Product Safety Australia, 2026) and no allergens in activities (National Allergy Council, 2026).
