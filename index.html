Two Critical Contexts for Early Childhood Practice

Young children develop within complex systems of relationships, culture and circumstance. This portfolio examines two significant contexts that shape the everyday lives of many Australian children: parental mental illness and First Nations families. Drawing on Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory and family systems theory, it explores impacts, policy responses, and practical strategies that promote belonging, security and flourishing.

Health & Wellbeing

Parental Mental Illness

When a parent experiences a diagnosed mental health condition, the emotional availability and daily functioning that young children need for secure attachment can be disrupted. High-quality early childhood education becomes a vital protective factor.

Consistent, warm relationships in early childhood settings support emotional regulation and trust

🧠 Understanding the Context

Parental mental illness refers to one or both parents living with conditions such as depression, anxiety or bipolar disorder that affect emotional availability. Australia’s focus on the first 2000 days recognises the intergenerational nature of these effects. Bronfenbrenner’s microsystem (home) and mesosystem (home–service connections) highlight why strong partnerships between educators and families matter so deeply.

👶 Impact on Children & Families

Children may develop insecure or disorganised attachment patterns, challenges with emotional regulation, language development and trust. Secondary stressors — economic pressure, isolation and disrupted routines — often compound the picture. Australia’s Early Development Census shows elevated vulnerability in social competence and emotional maturity for these children. Yet many children also demonstrate remarkable resilience when supported by consistent, non-judgmental relationships outside the home.

📋 Australian Policy Responses

The National Children’s Mental Health and Wellbeing Strategy and National Guidelines for mental health in early childhood health checks emphasise prevention, early detection and family-focused approaches within universal services. The Early Years Learning Framework V2.0 reinforces the link between children’s wellbeing and their family context, positioning educators as important contributors to mental health literacy.

🤝 Community Partnerships

Key partners include Emerging Minds (formerly COPMI) and local Maternal and Child Health / perinatal mental health services. Joint professional development, clear referral pathways (with family consent) and shared case planning create continuity of support across home, health services and the early childhood setting.

Cultural & Diversity

First Nations Families

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander families hold rich kinship systems, languages and connections to Country. Early childhood practice that centres cultural safety, self-determination and genuine partnership supports identity, belonging and healing from intergenerational trauma.

Connection to Country and community is a powerful protective factor for First Nations children

🌏 Understanding the Context

First Nations families encompass diverse cultures, languages and kinship systems. The EYLF V2.0 places Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives at the centre of practice for all children. Ecological systems theory helps us see how macrosystem policies (past and present), community services and family/kin networks interact. Family systems theory needs adaptation to honour communal child-rearing responsibilities.

🌱 Impact on Children & Families

Strong cultural identity and connection to community are central protective factors for social and emotional wellbeing. When a child’s identity is acknowledged and celebrated in the learning environment, belonging, language and identity can flourish. Racism and intergenerational trauma remain significant challenges. High-quality, culturally competent services that are co-designed with community can offer restorative care and promote excellence.

📜 Policy Landscape

The National Agreement on Closing the Gap, the National Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Early Childhood Strategy, and the embedding of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives in the EYLF V2.0 guide practice. Place-based initiatives such as Connected Beginnings demonstrate integrated approaches led by community. The ongoing challenge is moving from symbolic inclusion to genuine power-sharing and cultural safety.

🤝 Community Partnerships

SNAICC – National Voice for our Children and local Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisations (ACCOs) / Connected Beginnings sites are essential partners. Successful collaboration involves ongoing consultation, shared professional learning, culturally appropriate referral pathways that respect protocol, and employment of First Nations educators and staff.

Evidence-Informed Strategies for Practice

Family-Centred & Attachment-Informed Practice

Especially powerful when working with families affected by parental mental illness.

  • Collaborative, individualised learning plans that treat parents as experts in their own lives
  • Consistent key educators and visual schedules to support predictability
  • Flexible communication when parents have limited capacity
  • Circle of Security principles: interpreting behaviour as a call for connection, staying present during stress, and translating the child’s emotional world into everyday language for parents

Culturally Responsive & Anti-Bias Practice

Essential for genuine partnership with First Nations families and all children.

  • Embedding Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives through the 8 Ways of Learning rather than token events
  • Storytelling, non-verbal learning, land-based experiences and morning yarn circles as everyday practice
  • Critically examining the learning environment for accurate, contemporary representation
  • Actively challenging stereotypes and reflecting on personal bias

Moving Forward Together

Working effectively with families affected by parental mental illness and with First Nations families requires educators to see each child within their unique cultural and relational context. Ecological systems theory and family systems theory provide powerful lenses for this work.

Australian policy increasingly calls for family-centred and culturally safe practice. Real impact, however, rests on the capacity, confidence and commitment of individual educators and services — and on genuine partnerships with families and community.

Through individualised planning, attachment-informed approaches, culturally responsive pedagogy, authentic collaboration and carefully chosen children’s literature and resources, early childhood educators can foster children’s self-awareness, empathy, resilience and deep sense of belonging.
