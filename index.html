A practical portfolio for working with children in economic & social contexts

Children don’t arrive at the centre gate as blank slates. They arrive carrying their family’s circumstances — what happened at home last night, whether there was breakfast, whether the family is moving again next month. This portfolio turns research, policy and theory into Tuesday-morning practice.

Why this matters for educators

Early childhood services are one of the few universal touchpoints in a young child’s life. We see children several days a week, often for years — close enough to notice when something changes, and trusted enough that families sometimes tell us things they haven’t told anyone else.

That’s a privilege and a responsibility. The Early Years Learning Framework names equity, inclusion and high expectations as a principle, not an optional extra (AGDE, 2022).

A note on language

A child isn’t “a poverty child” or “a foster kid”. A child is experiencing financial hardship, or living in out-of-home care. The words we use in the staff room shape how we speak to and about children in the room.

Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model reminds us that systems furthest from the child — the rental market, welfare settings, government policy — can shape daily life as powerfully as the people right beside them (Grace et al., 2022).

Economic Understanding the context

Economic hardship covers more than a low bank balance. It includes income poverty (commonly 50% of median household income), housing stress (low-income households spending more than 30% of income on rent/mortgage), material deprivation, and financial precarity from casual, gig or seasonal work.

A family can look fine from the outside and still be in real trouble. Two working parents on casual contracts, paying Brisbane rents, can be one broken car away from crisis.

How big is this in Australia?

National

1 in 7 people · 1 in 6 children

3.7 million people below the poverty line in 2022–23, including 757,000 children (Davidson & Bradbury, 2025).

Housing

Brisbane median rent $643

Now consumes 29% of average renting household income — up from 22% five years earlier (SGS, 2025).

Assistance gap

43% still in rental stress

Low-income households receiving Commonwealth Rent Assistance at June 2025 (Productivity Commission, 2026).

Sociological theory — Bronfenbrenner in practice

Microsystem
Fewer books and toys; crowded or unstable housing; a tired, stretched parent.
Mesosystem
A parent on night shift can’t attend the meeting — the home–service link weakens.
Exosystem
Insecure roster, local rental market, closure of a bulk-billing GP.
Macrosystem
Welfare rates, minimum wage, Child Care Subsidy design.
Chronosystem
Post-2020 rental crisis and sustained cost-of-living pressure redefine “normal” hardship.

The Family Stress Model (Conger & Donnellan, 2007) shows the mechanism: economic pressure → parental distress → less warm, consistent parenting → impact on the child’s development. Supporting the parent is also supporting the child. Conflict and social reproduction perspectives (Grace & Baird, 2022) remind us Australian inequity is structural, not accidental.

Toxic stress research is clear: prolonged adversity without a buffering adult relationship can affect developing brain architecture. The key word is buffering — and an educator can be that buffer.

Impact on children and families

Development & learning

In the 2024 AEDC, 23.5% of children were developmentally vulnerable in one or more domains (up from 22% in 2021). In the most disadvantaged communities, about three in ten are vulnerable in one domain (Mitchell Institute, 2025).

  • Language — fewer books, less shared reading when parents are exhausted or working multiple jobs
  • Physical health — inconsistent nutrition, delayed dental/health checks, poor sleep
  • Social & emotional maturity — domains showing record vulnerability nationally
  • Executive function — planning, holding instructions and inhibiting impulses are stress-sensitive

Wellbeing & relationships

  • Shame & exclusion — children notice who has the excursion money and the character lunchbox
  • Attendance — housing instability means moves, broken friendships and broken continuity
  • Parental capacity — financial stress is strongly linked with anxiety and low mood
  • Withdrawal — families under stress often pull back from the supports they need

Service implications

  • Bond, upfront fees and subsidy paperwork are real barriers
  • Hidden costs (excursions, “bring a plate”, photo packs) quietly sort families
  • Universal provision is more dignified — and more effective — than targeted “help”

Social policy & Australian responses

All CCS-eligible families can access three days of subsidised ECEC per week (72 hours a fortnight), replacing the activity test. Families caring for First Nations children can access 100 hours per fortnight. This dismantles a catch-22 that hit low-income families hardest and is framed as a step toward universal ECEC.
Launched 7 May 2024 with a First Action Plan and Outcomes Framework (Dec 2024). Commits the Commonwealth to child-centred policy and better service integration, with explicit attention to families facing disadvantage.
Working: removing the activity test is genuine structural change; the Strategy signals a whole-of-government view of children.

Gaps: access ≠ participation (vacancies, transport, location still matter); gap fees remain a barrier; Australia has no official poverty line and no national child poverty target.

Framework levers: EYLF V2.0 principle of Equity, Inclusion and High Expectations, and NQS Quality Area 6 (collaborative partnerships with families and communities).

Strategies for practice

Strategy 1 — Universal, low-cost, non-stigmatising provision

Design the program so participation never depends on a family’s ability to pay, and no child is visibly identified as “the one who can’t”.

Why it works: if hardship harms partly through shame and exclusion, removing points of exclusion removes part of the harm. Aligns with EYLF equity and Grace & Baird’s structural argument.
  • Run a hidden-cost audit for a whole term — remove or absorb what you can
  • Service supplies everything for incursions, dress-ups and cooking (including spare hats, clothes, shoes as normal stock)
  • Rethink celebrations — shared cooking instead of “bring a plate”; educator portraits instead of paid photographers
  • Build curriculum around open-ended and natural materials
  • Quiet food shelf framed as “help yourself — for everyone”
  • Discreet enrolment support with myGov / subsidy claims
  • Free borrowing library of books and games

Strategy 2 — Buffering relationships + intentional language & literacy

A consistent key educator for each child, combined with deliberate high-quality language and early literacy experiences.

Why it works: buffering relationships moderate the effects of chronic stress on brain architecture; language is where socioeconomic gaps appear earliest and where quality ECE has the clearest effect.
  • Assign a key educator and protect it in rostering
  • Serve-and-return conversation; dialogic reading in small groups
  • Narrate and expand children’s language
  • Book bags home with a simple note in the family’s home language
  • Predictable rhythm — visual schedules, consistent routines, clear transition warnings
  • Notice changes, not deficits — respond with curiosity and a gentle family check-in

Social Children living in out-of-home care

Out-of-home care is care for children under 18 who cannot safely live with their parents. In Australia it includes relative/kinship care (largest category), foster care, residential care, and independent living for older young people. In Queensland this sits under the Department of Child Safety, Seniors and Disability Services and the Child Protection Act 1999 (Qld). Educators are mandatory reporters.

National snapshot

44,900 in care

At 30 June 2024 · 59,900 on care and protection orders · ~179,000 in contact with the system (2023–24).

Youngest children

Highest entry under 1

6.5 per 1,000 under one year — compared with ≤2.0 for all other age groups. Entry is concentrated in the years we work with.

Home-based care

~88%

About 39,700 children in foster, kinship or other home-based arrangements.

First Nations overrepresentation — not a side note

Of 44,900 children in care, 20,000 were Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander — a rate of 50 per 1,000 versus 4.6 for non-Indigenous children. In 2024 the rate was 11 times higher (up from 9.6 times in 2020). The Productivity Commission links this to intergenerational trauma, systemic racism, inequality in social determinants, and policies that operate to the detriment of First Nations people. Assessments of neglect are sometimes not culturally informed.

Our own notifications and observations are not culturally neutral. What we call concerning may be culturally different rather than unsafe.

Theory that guides practice

Attachment
Internal working models form from earliest caregiving. Disruption explains rejection of comfort or indiscriminate seeking. Consistency revises the model (Bowlby, 1988).
Bioecological
Entering care replaces home, often service, neighbourhood and peers at once — and adds an exosystem of caseworkers, court and contact visits.
Family systems
A child in care is still psychologically part of their birth family. Reunification is the goal for many; siblings and grandparents remain significant.
Trauma-informed
Behaviour that reads as defiance or blankness is frequently a survival response. Start from what happened to this child (Cashmore et al., 2022).
Postcolonial
The Stolen Generations and today’s system share institutional lineage — which is why the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Child Placement Principle exists (SNAICC, 2019).

Impact on children and families

Development & learning

  • Early neglect affects language input at the most sensitive point — many children enter care with delays
  • Executive function (planning, attention, impulse control) is vulnerable to chronic stress
  • Good news: development can improve significantly in stable foster care; stability is the active ingredient (Goemans et al., 2015)

Wellbeing

  • Grief and loss are constant — even when removal was necessary
  • Attachment behaviours can be confusing (indiscriminate affection, refusal of comfort, or both)
  • Contact visits are emotionally enormous; regulation often shifts either side
  • Identity questions and, for First Nations children, disconnection from culture, Country and community

Implications for services

  • Enrolment complexity — parental responsibility, authorised collection, information sharing; document and handle privately
  • Confidentiality — care status is private; never staff-room conversation or explanation to other parents
  • Curriculum landmines — family trees, baby photos, Mother’s/Father’s Day need redesign, not cancellation
  • Mandatory reporting — know Queensland obligations and act on suspicion
  • Interagency work — care team meetings, objective observations, Child Safety communication
  • Stability as core business — advocate for continued enrolment when placement changes

Social policy & Australian responses

National framework shifting emphasis from crisis response toward prevention and early intervention, with a dedicated Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander First Action Plan. Supports Closing the Gap Target 12.
Aim: reduce overrepresentation of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children in OOHC by 45% by 2031 (2019 baseline 54.2 per 1,000). Current trend is moving in the wrong direction.
Five elements — prevention, partnership, placement, participation, connection — with a hierarchy prioritising family, kin and community. National Principles for Child Safe Organisations and Queensland child safe standards require policies, reportable conduct processes, and a culture where children are listened to.

Gaps: Target 12 is not on track; prevention remains under-resourced relative to statutory response; recent AIHW data has Queensland gaps due to system upgrades.

Strategies for practice

Strategy 1 — Trauma-informed, relationship-based practice with a consistent key educator

One named educator holds primary responsibility for care and connection, working from a “what happened to you” frame, with predictability and co-regulation built into the day.

Why it works: attachment models revise through repeated responsive experience; meta-analytic evidence links developmental improvement to placement stability; consistency is protective (Cashmore et al., 2022).
  • Protect key educator continuity in rostering
  • Regulate before you reason — proximity, calm, rhythm first
  • Visual schedules, consistent routines, warning before every transition
  • Genuine safe base (soft, low-stimulus corner without it being a consequence)
  • Watch for triggers (noise, mealtimes, unfamiliar adults, days around contact visits)
  • Rupture and repair — stay calm and stay
  • Reflective supervision and debriefing for educators (vicarious trauma is real)
  • Brief daily handover with carers on sleep, food and mood

Strategy 2 — Identity, belonging and culturally safe life-story practice

Actively support the child’s sense of who they are, where they come from and who belongs to them — including birth family, carers, siblings, community and culture.

Why it works: EYLF Outcome 1 (strong sense of identity) is at risk for children in care. For First Nations children, connection is a protective factor and a policy requirement under the Child Placement Principle.
  • Redesign “family” curriculum → “my important people” circles
  • Reframe Mother’s/Father’s Day as “someone special”; check with carers quietly each year
  • Make baby photo requests optional with alternatives
  • Memory box / portfolio that travels with the child
  • Follow cultural support plans; partner with local Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisations
  • Use the child’s own language for family; answer honestly at the child’s level
  • Include carers as parents — invite them fully, no doorstep explanations required
